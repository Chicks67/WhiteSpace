# Blind brief comparison: rating sheet

## How to rate (please read this first)

**What this is.** Imagine an independent founder about to open a restaurant or cafe in New York City.
For each of the 10 cases below, two positioning briefs (Brief A and Brief B) were written for the same
concept in the same neighbourhood. They were made in different ways, and the order was shuffled at
random, so A is not always the same method. Your job is to judge which brief would help that founder
more, and to score each one.

**Time.** About 30 minutes. Please do it in one sitting if you can.

**Ground rules.**
1. Rate on your own. Please don't discuss the briefs with anyone (including the other team member) until you have
   sent your ratings.
2. Judge only what is written. Don't search the web, and don't try to work out how each brief was made.
3. Use what you already know about NYC. If a brief names a place or brand you know is wrong for that
   area, mark it down on plausibility. If you don't recognise a name, don't penalise it for that alone.
4. Length is roughly equal by design, so don't reward a brief for being longer.

**For each case, give four things.**

1. **Preference: A, B or tie.** Which brief would you rather hand to that founder? Use tie only if you
   genuinely cannot choose.
2. **Specificity** (score each brief 1 to 5): is it written for *this* neighbourhood?
   - 1 = could be pasted onto any neighbourhood in NYC
   - 3 = some real local detail, but much of it is generic
   - 5 = clearly about this neighbourhood; most of it would not fit elsewhere
3. **Plausibility** (1 to 5): does it sound true and workable for this area?
   - 1 = contains claims, names or advice that seem wrong or made up
   - 3 = mostly believable, with a few doubtful points
   - 5 = everything sounds credible for this place and concept
4. **Usefulness** (1 to 5): could the founder act on it?
   - 1 = nothing they could act on
   - 3 = some useful ideas, but they would still need to work most of it out
   - 5 = concrete enough to act on this week (who to target, what to put on the menu, who to call)

Use the whole 1 to 5 range; 2 and 4 are fine for in-between.

**How to reply.** Reply to the email this came in, with one line per case, in this exact format:

`case: preference, A specificity/plausibility/usefulness, B specificity/plausibility/usefulness`

Example: `1: B, A 3/4/3, B 4/4/5` means case 1, you prefer B; Brief A scored 3 for specificity,
4 for plausibility, 3 for usefulness; Brief B scored 4, 4, 5. You can add a short comment after a `#`
if you want, for example `1: B, A 3/4/3, B 4/4/5  # B's partner ideas were much more concrete`.

Copy and fill in this template:

```

4:

9:
10:
```

Thank you!

## Case 1: Korean cafe in Flushing, Queens

### Brief A

Audience

Serve Flushing residents, Korean families, Asian American young professionals, students, and shoppers seeking a comfortable daytime meeting place. Welcome multigenerational groups while making the room easy for solo coffee breaks, study sessions, and casual dates.

Brand voice

Warm, contemporary, and quietly confident. Use clear Korean and English naming, explaining unfamiliar ingredients without turning the brand into a lesson. Communicate craft, hospitality, and neighborhood familiarity rather than trend-chasing.

Menu emphasis

Build around excellent coffee, Korean teas, and restrained cafe food. Lead with dalgona-style drinks, yuja tea, omija ade, misugaru, and seasonal fruit beverages. Pair them with toast, egg sandwiches, kimbap, savory pastries, and desserts such as bingsu, yakgwa, and soboro-inspired baked goods. Offer fast counter service, vegetarian choices, and giftable packaged items.

Music and decor

Use low-volume Korean city pop, indie, jazz, and acoustic playlists during the day. Create a bright, calm interior with pale wood, stainless steel, handmade ceramics, and subtle references to Korean typography or market signage. Provide communal tables, outlets, stroller-friendly circulation, and a few intimate two-tops.

Launch partners

Coordinate opening-week sampling with H Mart Flushing and nearby Korean grocers. Invite the Flushing BID, Queens College student organizations, and local Korean-American creators for preview events. Cross-promote with neighborhood bakeries, florists, and gift shops rather than relying only on paid influencers.

### Brief B

Audience

Target Flushing’s solo diners, students, shoppers, office workers, and small groups seeking an easy Korean café stop rather than a formal meal. Welcome Chinese- and Japanese-speaking guests alongside Korean customers with clear trilingual signage, quick ordering, and seating that supports both brief visits and laptop stays. Make the experience approachable for people already comfortable with Asian, sushi, and dessert-focused dining.

Brand voice

Use a warm, modern, lightly playful voice: Korean first in spirit, but never insider-only. Explain unfamiliar items in plain English and Chinese, with concise menu descriptions and confident recommendations. Name signature drinks and desserts memorably, while keeping service polished and efficient.

Menu emphasis

Lead with excellent coffee, Korean teas, dalgona-style drinks, fruit ades, and bingsu designed for sharing or solo ordering. Add compact savory items such as Korean toast, kimbap, and a crisp Korean fried-chicken bite. Offer a focused pastry case with rotating Korean-inspired flavors, plus one dependable chocolate or croissant option for broad appeal.

Music and decor

Pair contemporary Korean R&B, indie, and mellow house with warm wood, stainless accents, soft lighting, and a visible dessert counter. Create communal and two-top seating, plentiful outlets, and an uncluttered photo moment without making the room feel staged.

Launch partners

Pursue opening-week sampling with H Mart, cross-promotions with nearby Korean retailers, and a neighborhood listing push through Yelp. Explore receipt-based offers with Gyu-Kaku and dessert collaborations with Lady M.


## Case 2: Korean cafe in Williamsburg, Brooklyn

### Brief A

Audience

Target design-conscious Williamsburg residents, creative professionals, and visitors seeking an elevated but approachable Korean café. Build for solo coffee runs, casual dates, weekend brunch, and early evening drinks rather than formal dining. Welcome laptop users during daytime, while creating enough energy for social visits after work.

Brand voice

Warm, clever, and culturally confident. Explain Korean ingredients without over-teaching or turning the experience into a theme. Use concise menu language, playful signage, and a tone that feels contemporary, hospitable, and distinctly Brooklyn.

Menu emphasis

Lead with excellent coffee, matcha, and Korean tea alongside a focused all-day menu: egg-and-gim breakfast sandwiches, kimchi grilled cheese, savory rice bowls, and seasonal banchan plates. Offer hotteok, yakgwa, and Korean cream buns with pastries from Ovenly or a comparable local baker. Add natural wine, soju cocktails, and a small beer selection for a daytime-to-evening transition.

Music and decor

Create a bright, tactile room with pale wood, stainless steel, handmade ceramics, and one strong color accent. Commission work from a Brooklyn ceramicist and use furniture or objects with the considered feel of Mociun or Sandy Liang. Play Korean indie, city pop, R&B, and mellow house at conversational volume; increase tempo modestly after 6 p.m.

Launch partners

Explore a coffee collaboration with La Colombe, a pastry pop-up with Ovenly, and a capsule or opening event with Lisa Says Gah or Saturdays NYC. Add a neighborhood vinyl night and a Korean beverage tasting with a local distributor.

### Brief B

Audience
Target Williamsburg residents, freelancers and weekend visitors seeking excellent coffee with a distinctly Korean food offering. Position the cafe as an everyday neighborhood ritual, not a novelty dessert destination. Build repeat visits around breakfast and an afternoon snack.

Brand voice
Warm, assured and lightly playful. Keep Korean dish names, followed by short, appetizing English descriptions. Let the founder’s personal connection shape the storytelling; avoid generic Seoul-inspired language. Lead with flavor and hospitality rather than explaining every tradition.

Menu emphasis
Anchor the menu with espresso, barley tea and a signature black sesame latte. Pair egg-and-cheese toast on milk bread with a kimchi option, compact lunch gimbap and injeolmi-inspired baked goods. Offer one seasonal bingsu for afternoon visits. Start tight: shared ingredients, fast assembly and clear sesame, soy and dairy labeling.

Music and decor
Mix Korean indie, mellow R&B and understated jazz at conversation volume. Use warm wood, stainless accents and locally made ceramics. Combine a communal table with laptop-free two-tops during peak hours.

Launch partners
Approach Williamsburg-based Partners Coffee for wholesale supply and barista training. Pitch McNally Jackson’s Williamsburg bookstore a Korean-literature reading with cafe refreshments. Invite neighborhood ceramicists to sell limited-run mugs.


## Case 3: Mexican taqueria in Jackson Heights, Queens

### Brief A

Audience

Serve Jackson Heights residents first: Mexican families, Latin American neighbors, transit commuters, and Queens diners seeking an affordable, high-quality taco stop. Build for regulars rather than destination-only visitors, with generous portions, fast service, and options for families, late shifts, and takeout.

Brand voice

Warm, direct, and proudly neighborhood-rooted. Use clear Spanish-English communication without forced slang or caricature. Tell the story through regional specificity: the founder’s influences, masa preparation, sourcing, and the people behind the counter.

Menu emphasis

Make nixtamalized-corn tacos the signature, with a tight menu of regional specialties: al pastor, carnitas, barbacoa, tinga, and a strong vegetarian option such as huitlacoche or mushroom adobo. Offer handmade tortillas, salsas with distinct heat levels, aguas frescas, and a few family-style combinations. Price transparently and keep ordering simple.

Music and decor

Use cumbia, sonidero, norteño, and contemporary Mexican selections at conversational volume. Create a colorful but restrained room: tile, warm wood, papel picado used selectively, framed neighborhood photography, and an open tortilla or prep counter. Avoid generic “fiesta” decor.

Launch partners

Coordinate with the Jackson Heights Merchants Association and Make the Road New York for a community preview. Explore a tortilla collaboration with Tortilleria Nixtamal in Corona, and a pop-up or sampling event with Queens Night Market.

### Brief B

Audience
Make this a neighborhood taqueria for Jackson Heights regulars: friends meeting after work, families sharing an early dinner, and weekend visitors exploring Queens. Prioritize repeat visits with approachable prices, fast pickup, and tables that accommodate groups.

Brand voice
Warm, direct, and proudly Mexican. Use clear Spanish-English menu descriptions, explain regional specialties without lecturing, and let the founder’s actual roots shape the story. Lead with “your usual, plus something worth trying,” not destination-dining hype.

Menu emphasis
Build around excellent al pastor, carnitas, and mushroom tacos, with one rotating regional special. Offer a taco-and-drink lunch combination and shareable taco platters. Keep tortillas central; specify whether they’re house-made or name the supplier. Pair Mexican lagers with a Montauk Brewing option, plus strong aguas frescas.

Music and decor
Choose contemporary Mexican and Latin playlists at conversation-friendly volume. Use warm lighting, durable communal tables, and commissioned work from Queens artists. Keep one screen for major soccer matches, not constant sports coverage.

Launch partners
Approach nearby merchants for reciprocal offers and Queens-based soccer leagues for post-match bookings. Explore Queens Museum staff catering and Montauk Brewing launch support. Pay local musicians for a small opening-week set.


## Case 4: Coffee shop in Astoria, Queens

### Brief A

Audience

Build for Astoria residents who want an everyday coffee stop with neighborhood character: morning commuters, hybrid workers and friends meeting on weekends. Prioritize repeat visits over destination hype, with quick pickup and seating that welcomes conversation.

Brand voice

Warm, direct and lightly playful. Sound like a helpful neighbor, not a coffee lecturer. Explain beans through familiar flavors, post prices clearly and introduce staff by name. Make “your regular, made right” the guiding promise.

Menu emphasis

Lead with excellent espresso, freddo espresso and freddo cappuccino alongside dependable filter coffee. Pair Greek-inspired orange cake and spinach pie with a tight pastry selection. Offer one substantial savory sandwich, a smaller sweet treat and clear dairy-free options. Keep the opening menu compact enough for fast service.

Music and decor

Use warm wood, durable café tables and rotating work by Queens artists. Mix mellow soul, jazz and contemporary Greek tracks at conversation-friendly volume. Separate laptop-friendly counter seats from small social tables.

Launch partners

Approach a nearby Greek bakery for pastry supply and a Queens roaster such as Mighty Oak for wholesale discussions. Invite Astoria Bookshop to explore a coffee-and-reading event; offer nearby studios a trackable opening-week voucher.

### Brief B

Audience

Astoria residents, remote workers, commuters, and neighborhood regulars seeking excellent coffee without a precious, exclusive atmosphere. Prioritize customers who value quality, comfortable seating, dependable Wi-Fi, and a place that feels distinctly local rather than interchangeable.

Brand voice

Warm, observant, and lightly witty. Speak like a knowledgeable neighbor: clear about sourcing and craft, never overly technical. Use Queens references sparingly and authentically, with hospitality taking precedence over lifestyle branding.

Menu emphasis

Build around espresso, batch brew, and well-made iced coffee, supported by rotating single-origin options and an accessible house blend. Add Greek coffee or a seasonal Balkan-inspired special as a respectful Astoria nod. Source pastries from Martha’s Country Bakery or a nearby independent baker, with strong savory breakfast options and several genuinely satisfying non-dairy choices.

Music and decor

Create a bright, comfortable room with durable wood, local art, plants, and flexible seating for solo work and conversation. Keep music at conversation level: soul, jazz, global grooves, and contemporary Queens DJs. Feature rotating work from Astoria artists or students, avoiding generic “industrial” café styling.

Launch partners

Host opening-week collaborations with Astoria Bookshop, local ceramicists, and Queens-based artists. Offer a pastry pop-up with Martha’s Country Bakery, a community give-back with Astoria Food Pantry, and a neighborhood DJ morning curated by a local selector. Consider a coffee-roasting partnership with Partners Coffee or Variety Coffee Roasters.


## Case 5: Caribbean restaurant in Flatbush, Brooklyn

### Brief A

Audience

Build for Flatbush locals first: Caribbean families, younger creatives, service workers, and groups looking for a lively meal rather than a formal night out. Welcome visitors without turning the restaurant into a tourist attraction. Make the room comfortable for solo diners, dates, birthdays, and post-work gatherings.

Brand voice

Confident, warm, and proudly diasporic. Use straightforward language with moments of Jamaican, Haitian, Trinidadian, and broader Caribbean expression, but avoid forced slang. Present the brand as contemporary Brooklyn hospitality with deep cultural roots.

Menu emphasis

Lead with expertly seasoned jerk chicken, curry goat, oxtail, doubles, roti, patties, and seafood. Make wings a signature, with sauces such as jerk honey, Scotch bonnet mango, and tamarind pepper. Offer generous shareable plates, strong vegetarian options, and lunch-friendly combos. Build a focused rum, sorrel, ginger beer, and tropical cocktail program.

Music and decor

Create an energetic lounge atmosphere: East Coast hip-hop, dancehall, soca, kompa, reggae, and R&B, with volume moderated during dinner. Use warm wood, saturated Caribbean colors, vintage travel graphics, local photography, and subtle references to Brooklyn street culture.

Launch partners

Collaborate with MOCAAD on an opening exhibition or artist program, The Brooklyn Circus on staff capsules or merchandise, and local DJs for rotating nights. Explore a Digicel Jamaica viewing event around football, plus Flatbush Caribbean grocers and cultural organizations for tastings and community previews.

### Brief B

Audience

Build for Flatbush’s Caribbean families, second-generation Brooklynites, nearby students, and food-curious diners seeking an energetic neighborhood destination. Welcome both quick weekday customers and groups celebrating birthdays, Sunday meals, and cultural occasions. Price the menu accessibly, with enough premium dishes and drinks to support dinner occasions.

Brand voice

Confident, warm, and proudly specific. Use familiar Caribbean language without turning culture into costume. Tell clear stories about islands, techniques, and family traditions; keep copy conversational, witty, and Brooklyn-aware. Avoid generic “tropical” clichés.

Menu emphasis

Lead with craveable staples: jerk chicken, curry goat, oxtail, roti, doubles, rice and peas, fried plantains, and escovitch fish. Offer mix-and-match plates for lunch, generous family bundles, and strong vegetarian choices such as ital stew, callaloo, and pumpkin. Add sorrel, mauby, ginger beer, rum cocktails, and a concise natural-wine or beer list.

Music and decor

Program reggae, soca, dancehall, kompa, calypso, and Afrobeat, balancing recognizable classics with local DJs. Create a colorful but mature room using wood, woven textures, vintage Caribbean travel posters, and artwork commissioned from Brooklyn artists. Keep lighting flattering and music conversation-friendly during dinner.

Launch partners

Collaborate with Flatbush Junction BID, nearby Caribbean grocers and bakeries, Brooklyn-based DJs, and Caribbean cultural organizations for opening events. Cross-promote with platforms such as BKLYN HOUSE Hotel and local food creators; use neighborhood flyers, church networks, and community markets to drive first visits.
